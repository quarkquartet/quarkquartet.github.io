Accelerator experiments allow us to produce new particles in the laboratory and study their interactions and decays.
Their discovery potential depends on the available energy, the number of collisions, and the detector's ability to distinguish a signal from background.
High-energy colliders can extend the search to heavier particles, while intense beams striking fixed targets provide access to light particles with very small couplings.

My research develops search strategies and evaluates the discovery potential of proposed and operating experiments.
I focus on two directions: future lepton colliders and beam-dump searches.

## Future lepton colliders

Lepton colliders bring beams of electrons or muons into collision.
Their well-characterized initial state and relatively clean backgrounds compared with hadron colliders allow precise studies of the particles produced and the interactions responsible for their production.

These machines probe new physics in two complementary ways.
When enough energy is available, new particles can be produced directly and identified through their decay products or the missing energy carried by invisible particles.
Precision measurements can also probe heavier particles through their effects on processes involving known particles.

Well-known future lepton collider proposals include, but not limited to, high-energy muon colliders and FCC-ee.
Another proposal is [$\mu$TRISTAN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.06664), developed in Japan, which would collide a $1~\mathrm{TeV}$ $\mu^+$ beam with either another $\mu^+$ beam of the same energy or a $30~\mathrm{GeV}$ $e^-$ beam.
This facility offers distinctive opportunities to search for new physics and has a relatively promising timeline among future collider proposals.

My research explores how future lepton colliders can probe extensions of the Higgs and gauge sectors.

## Beam dumps

Beam-dump experiments direct an intense particle beam into a thick target.
The collisions and the decays of particles produced in the target can create light new particles, including dark-sector mediators.
Material downstream absorbs much of the ordinary collision debris, while weakly interacting particles can travel through it and reach a detector.
The large number of beam particles makes these experiments sensitive to rare production processes and small couplings for particles with masses in the MeV-GeV range.

My work develops search strategies for light new particles at beam-dump and fixed-target experiments.
For example, we showed that MUonE's precise tracking enables displaced-vertex searches for [dark photons](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.08843), [other vector bosons](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.03222), and [thermal relic dark matter](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00170).
